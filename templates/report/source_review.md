# Single-Source Evidence Review

## Intended Use

Use this structure when at most one citable source is available. It is a
critical review of supplied material, not a literature-wide survey or a new
experimental paper. Keep it concise and match the source's actual evidentiary
strength. Never invent a competing method family or an observed baseline.

## Writing Workflow

Draft supported claims and limitations before the summary. Cite the source near
each source-specific claim. If the source is merely a proposal or task note,
present it as such; do not turn it into an empirical finding.

Draft order: Claims And Evidence -> Evaluation Status -> Limitations And Open Questions -> Conclusion -> Scope And Source -> Summary

## Summary

State the question, what the single supplied source actually establishes, and
the most important uncertainty. Avoid claims of consensus or generalization.

## Scope And Source

Identify the source and the question it addresses. Distinguish a published
study, an implementation note, a proposal, and a local task fixture. State
which parts of the source were available without describing pipeline internals.

## Claims And Evidence

Separate the source's stated ideas, any reported observations, and the
reviewer's inferences. Use a short evidence table or bullets only if it helps
readability. Do not infer an experiment or comparator from a proposed test.

## Evaluation Status

Describe evaluations actually reported by the source, including conditions,
metrics, and limitations. If none were reported, say so briefly and identify
what a future test would need to measure. Do not repeat an imaginary protocol
as if it were an observed comparison.

## Limitations And Open Questions

Summarize uncertainty, missing evidence, and what additional sources or runs
would change the conclusion. Do not restate the same missing facts in every
section.

## Conclusion

Give the bounded takeaway and a justified next step. Keep proposed work
distinct from the source's findings.
